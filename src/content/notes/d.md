The three of us moved with quiet efficiency, a lifetime of trust compressed into urgent motion. She packed a single duffel bag, selecting items with the calculated precision of someone accustomed to quick departures. Photographs, a leather-bound journal, a small wooden box I didn't recognize—these went in first, followed by practical necessities. "Forty minutes," he said, checking his watch as he returned from scouting the building's rear exit. "There's a service alley that leads to the next street over." I stood by the window, watching the chocolate car through a narrow gap in the blinds. A different driver now sat behind the wheel, face obscured by sunglasses despite the overcast sky that had rolled in during the last hour. The change in weather felt symbolic, as if the universe itself acknowledged the gravity of our collective decision. "We need transportation," I said, turning away from the window. "Something untraceable." She zipped her bag closed with a decisive gesture. "My neighbor downstairs. The old man with the hearing aid who pretends not to have one." A hint of a smile softened her features. "He owes me a favor. Three, actually." "For what?" he asked, ever practical. "I wrote letters to his estranged daughter. Found the right words when he couldn't." She shouldered her bag. "His Buick isn't much, but it's registered under his late wife's maiden name. It'll buy us time." The apartment looked strangely bare, though she'd taken so little. I realized the difference was in her—the careful facade had dropped away, revealing the sharper, more vibrant person we'd glimpsed in fragments since arriving. The plastic wrappings we'd discarded lay forgotten in the corner, a metaphor too obvious to acknowledge. "Twenty-five minutes," he announced, slinging her duffel over his shoulder alongside his own backpack. "We should move." She took one last sweeping glance around the apartment. "The hard drive," she said suddenly, ducking into the bedroom. She returned seconds later with a small external drive. "Everything I've ever written under my own name. Before... all this." The recognition hit me with unexpected force—this was the part of herself she'd preserved all these years, hidden away from the invisible architects of the chocolate car. Her true voice, untainted by the machinery of influence she'd become part of. We slipped down the service stairs single file, our footsteps echoing in the narrow concrete passage. No words passed between us; we'd already said everything necessary in the frantic planning of the last hour. At the first-floor landing, she knocked on a weathered door with a distinct pattern—two quick, one slow, two quick again. The old man who answered looked unsurprised, as if midnight escapes were common in his world. Perhaps they were. He pressed keys into her palm, his gnarled fingers lingering against hers for a moment longer than necessary. "Northeast corner of Westlake Cemetery," he said, his voice a gravelly whisper. "Under the blue tarp. Full tank." She nodded, something unspoken passing between them. "The orchids need water twice—" "I know how to disappear too, dear," he interrupted gently. "Go." The service exit deposited us into an alley damp from recent rain, the smell of wet asphalt and garbage strangely invigorating. Freedom has its own particular scent. We moved quickly toward the cemetery five blocks north, maintaining separation as she'd instructed—him first, then her thirty seconds later, me bringing up the rear. The weight of surveillance pressed against my shoulders despite the empty streets. The Buick waited exactly where promised, an aging sedan the color of faded denim, inconspicuous to the point of invisibility. She slid behind the wheel while he took shotgun. I climbed into the back, sinking into upholstery that smelled of peppermints and old cologne. "Ten minutes to spare," I said as she guided the car through cemetery gates and onto the main road, moving away from the apartment at exactly the speed limit. "They'll know by now," she replied, eyes flicking to the rearview mirror. "The question is what they'll do about it." He turned in his seat to face me, his expression graver than I'd ever seen it. "Where we're going—it won't be simple. Are you sure about this?" The city scrolled past the windows, familiar streets suddenly foreign in the context of our escape. I thought about my apartment in Boston, my unfinished manuscript, the life I'd constructed with careful precision. Then I looked at them—these constants in my inconstant existence—and felt something click into place that had been misaligned for years. "I'm sure," I said, surprised by the steadiness in my voice. "Some stories you have to see through to the end." She caught my eyes in the rearview mirror, and for the first time since we'd arrived, her smile reached all the way to her eyes. "Then let's find out who we really are." She guided the Buick onto the highway ramp, accelerating into the stream of anonymous traffic heading west. Behind us, somewhere in the city's embrace, a chocolate car sat empty outside an abandoned apartment, its purpose unfulfilled. Ahead lay uncertainty, danger, reinvention—but also possibility. The three of us, bound by history and choice, moved forward into a future we would write together, one word at a time.
